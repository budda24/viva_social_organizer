The Shift

A new interface for using computers just emerged — and almost no company can touch it yet.

For forty years we operated computers through buttons someone else designed: menus, forms, dashboards. You adapted to the software. In 2025 that flipped. With agentic AI — Claude Code and its kind — you describe the outcome in plain language and the machine builds the path. The software adapts to you.

The old interface

You learn the tool. You click through someone else's screens. Anything it doesn't already do, you can't do — you wait for a vendor, a developer, a quarter.

The new interface

You state the result. An agent reads your files, touches your real systems, and assembles the workflow on the spot. The ceiling moves from "what's in the menu" to "what you can describe."

This is the biggest change in how we use computers since the graphical interface. The technology is here today — but for the average 30-person company it's a YouTube demo, not something running in their business.

The Problem

The new interface is real — but it's trapped on one developer's laptop.

A single skilled operator with an AI agent can now automate almost any repetitive office task. The capability exists. What's missing is the bridge between that operator and the team that actually does the work.

It lives locally

The real power needs real access — your files, your tools, your stack. That runs on a machine, behind a login. The team never sees it.

It dies with the builder

The automation lives in one person's head and terminal. They leave, get busy, or go on holiday — and the workflow stops.

The team can't run it

Marek in the office can't open a terminal. If it isn't a button he can press, with instructions he can follow, it may as well not exist.

Only 8.7% of Polish firms use AI (Eurostat 2024, second-lowest in the EU) — not for lack of tools, but for lack of a way to put them in non-technical hands.
